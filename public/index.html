No. 3 · HN

Isar Aerospace reaches orbit and deploys payloads on second flight

From linkIsar Aerospace says its second Spectrum flight lifted off from Andøya, Norway, reached orbit, and deployed payloads selected through the German Space Agency's Microlauncher Competition. The company calls itself the first European commercial launch provider to place satellites in orbit and presents the mission as a new sovereign option for European customers. The flight passed MaxQ, main-engine cutoff, stage separation, second-stage ignition, fairing jettison, circularization, and spacecraft separation; Isar now plans to scale its vertically integrated production system, where vehicles three through seven are already being built and a new factory is intended to support as many as 40 launches a year.

From commentsHN readers celebrated the technical milestone but immediately complicated the sovereignty claim. Much of the discussion compared Norway with established launches from French Guiana, which is both French and EU territory, and asked how independent a European rocket can be if any of its components remain subject to American ITAR export controls. Others contrasted Europe's cautious, lower-cadence launch culture with the faster trial-and-error model associated with SpaceX. The result was admiration for Isar's engineering paired with a sober debate about supply chains, launch geography, and what strategic autonomy actually requires.

No. 4 · HN

A/I shuts down — Stay human

From linkThe Autistici/Inventati collective announced that it will close after 25 years of providing secure, non-commercial, privacy-friendly digital services. The group says that after being designated a global terrorist organization it tried to keep operating, but concluded that doing so could expose users, maintainers, and their families to legal, financial, or extra-legal retaliation. It plans to publish backup instructions for blogs, mailboxes, and websites while warning that further disruptions may arrive without notice. The farewell is deliberately human rather than technical: the infrastructure is ending, but the collective asks its community to carry its resistance and relationships forward offline.

From commentsThe HN thread focused on the missing bridge between the collective's statement and the U.S. State Department's rationale. Some readers found the government release's ideological language and unsupported labels alarming, while others thought its allegations sounded serious enough to undermine any claim of neutral infrastructure. Commenters debated whether vetting users reflects complicity or a community's ordinary right to set boundaries, and whether designating a service because some users engaged in violence creates a precedent that could chill broad categories of political hosting. The dominant response was uncertainty sharpened by concern over due process and collective punishment.

No. 5 · HN

Doomscrolling ourselves to death

From linkEd West reviews James Marriott's The New Dark Ages through the anxiety many parents feel about children replacing books with algorithmic video and short-form feeds. The argument reaches further back than smartphones: drawing on Neil Postman, Marriott traces the erosion of sustained, reasoned public discourse to television's preference for novelty and personality, with phones intensifying a transformation already underway. Falling reading comprehension and steep declines in reading for pleasure are presented not as nostalgia for a canon but as a civic problem, because a culture less practiced at following long arguments becomes easier to entertain, provoke, and manipulate.

From commentsHN readers answered with personal accounts of damaged concentration, procrastination, and a new resistance to books or even a full film after years of passive Reddit, X, and YouTube use. Several compared infinite feeds to hyper-palatable food: difficult to resist, immediately rewarding, and poor nourishment for attention. People shared practical attempts to recover depth by blocking sites, deleting accounts, using e-readers, and deliberately finishing longer works, but the broader mood was pessimistic. Individual habits can improve, commenters said, while the social incentives and business models driving compulsive media remain much harder to escape.

No. 6 · HN

Alberta adds a per-panel solar recycling fee

From linkAlberta will add a $14 recycling fee to every solar panel sold beginning October 1 and prohibit discarded panels from entering landfills, directing them toward reuse and recycling instead. The government argues that planning now will prevent a future waste problem as today's installations reach the end of their 25-to-35-year lives. The article sees the fee as another barrier to renewables, citing industry claims that it is dramatically higher than comparable charges elsewhere, lacks a published cost breakdown, and arrives decades before a large waste stream exists. It also places the measure beside other provincial policies that critics say have chilled renewable investment.

From commentsThe HN discussion spent less time on the article's rhetoric than on calibrating the economics. Commenters calculated that a 12-kilowatt home system might use about 24 panels, making the fee roughly $336 or around five percent of the panels' pre-tax price. Some considered that meaningful but far from the headline's implication of a crippling tax; others argued the unexplained rate matters more than the absolute amount, especially when compounded with Alberta's previous pause and new rules for renewable projects. The thread ultimately split between legitimate end-of-life stewardship and suspicion that recycling is being used selectively as industrial policy.

No. 10 · HN

M-DISC and the promise of thousand-year optical storage

From linkM-DISC is a write-once DVD and Blu-ray format introduced in 2009 for archival storage, using an inert recording layer rather than the organic dyes associated with ordinary recordable media. Properly stored DVDs were marketed with a lifespan of up to a thousand years, and the discs remain readable in many conventional optical drives while offering capacities from 4.7 to 100 GB. The story is less simple for current products: the original company went bankrupt, later Blu-rays changed formulation, and modern Verbatim-branded M-DISCs use a metal-ablative layer also found in other discs. Laboratory projections remain impressive, but the thousand-year promise still extends far beyond direct observation.

From commentsHN's data archivists were skeptical of treating the M-DISC label as a magic guarantee. The most detailed replies said current products may be ordinary MABL Blu-rays sold at a premium, with realistic century-scale expectations rather than literal millennium durability, while Verbatim maintains that the newer formulation preserves the original longevity. Readers emphasized that no medium removes the need for multiple copies, checksums, careful storage, and periodic migration; drives and file formats may disappear before the disc fails. Even so, many valued optical media as a cheap, offline, ransomware-resistant layer in a broader backup plan.

No. 12 · HN

Asahi Linux officially supports Apple M3 Macs, with caveats

From linkAsahi Linux now officially supports most M3, M3 Pro, and M3 Max Macs, bringing the generation close to the hardware coverage already available on M1 and M2 machines. The milestone is qualified: 3D acceleration is not yet performant or power-efficient, sleep and MacBook HDMI output are missing because Display Coprocessor support remains incomplete, and the M3 Ultra Mac Studio is excluded. Basic M3 boot support has reached the mainline Linux kernel, but many patches still need upstreaming before standard kernels match Asahi's downstream build. For now, the release is a usable platform for enthusiasts rather than a frictionless replacement for macOS.

From commentsHN readers praised the Asahi team's reverse engineering as extraordinary while expressing frustration that it is necessary at all. Several compared the work to repairing a spaceship during launch and asked why Apple does not publish the documentation needed for another operating system. Others noted that a complete platform cannot be explained in a single PDF: even documented hardware can require thousands of pages, firmware knowledge, and sustained driver work. The thread's enthusiasm was therefore tempered by the caveats, especially graphics and sleep, but the prevailing view was that each supported generation expands the practical life and ownership of excellent hardware.
